# LLM-like patterns — 6 categories

## A. Rhetorical structure

- **Triad as climax**: three parallel elements used to close on a high note ("fast, reliable, and above all human"). An occasional triad is fine; the problem is using it systematically as a closing device.
- **"Not just X, but Y"**: almost always removable, rarely adds information.
- **Summary-style closing**: "In conclusion, this shows that..." / "Ultimately, this demonstrates...". If the reader has read the text, they don't need a closing recap.
- **Question-restating opener**: "Great question." / "When it comes to X..." — start with content, not an empty premise.

**Before (EN)**: "It's not just about speed, it's about trust. Ultimately, a faster app builds faster, more reliable, and more delightful experiences."
**After (EN)**: "A faster cold start matters because our Android base skews toward low-end devices, where every extra 200ms shows up in the retention numbers."

**Before (IT)**: "Non è solo una questione di performance, ma di esperienza utente nel suo complesso. In conclusione, ottimizzare il bundle porta velocità, manutenibilità e, soprattutto, felicità del team."
**After (IT)**: "Il bundle più leggero significa meno tempo di parsing su device di fascia bassa — che sono la maggioranza dei nostri utenti Android in Turchia."

## B. Lexicon / telltale words

**English**: delve, tapestry, leverage (as a generic verb), robust, landscape, underscore, navigate (complexity), foster, boundaries, unlock, seamless, holistic, testament to, plays a pivotal role, at the end of the day, in today's fast-paced world.

**Italian**: cruciale, fondamentale, cardine, sinergia, non a caso, in un mondo sempre più connesso/digitale, abbracciare (il cambiamento), navigare (la complessità), gettare le basi, un vero e proprio, a tutti gli effetti, concreto/concretezza used as filler, panorama (referring to an industry).

Practical rule: if a word would sound fine in a keynote opener but no one would use it chatting with a coworker over coffee, it's suspect.

## C. Punctuation

- **Em-dash (—)** used more than once every ~150 words as a systematic substitute for commas/periods. Occasional use is natural; repetition is the tell.
- **Bulleted lists** where flowing prose would work better — a typical symptom when the idea doesn't actually need a list but gets "chunked" to look organized.
- **Bold used as a summary**: bolding 3-4 key words per sentence, as if the reader were meant to scan rather than read.

## D. Hedging and filler

- "It's worth noting that...", "It is important to mention that...", plus their Italian equivalents "è importante notare che...", "vale la pena sottolineare che..." — the opening clause can almost always be dropped, starting straight from the content.
- Superfluous disclaimers added out of caution where the context doesn't call for them.

## E. Mechanical parallelism / rhythm

Consecutive sentences all of the same length, all starting with subject+verb, with no variation in cadence. Human prose has irregular rhythm: short sentences breaking up longer blocks, asides, sentences starting with a conjunction when it fits.

**Tell**: if you read the paragraph aloud and every sentence has the same "melody," that's a signal.

## F. Meta-commentary

- Announcing the action instead of doing it: "Let's now analyze...", "Let me break this down...", "I'll now explain...".
- Recapping what was just said at the end of a paragraph, as if the reader needed an immediate reminder.

---

Language note: the structural patterns (A, C, E, F) are shared across both languages; the lexical patterns (B) must be checked separately, since telltale words don't map 1:1 between languages.
